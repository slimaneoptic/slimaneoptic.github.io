How the score is made

  1. Collect the eventsEach transaction, the sender’s SIM swaps and device changes, and the recipient’s account. Only events from before the transaction are used.
  2. Turn them into 11 signalsTime since the last SIM swap and device change, the amount itself and against this sender’s usual, whether the recipient is new and how old their account is, recent activity, the sender’s history, and how usual the time of day is.
  3. Score from 0 to 100A model (XGBoost) learns fraud patterns from the earlier weeks of labelled transactions. The next week, which it did not learn from, is used to translate its raw output into the 0-100 score.
  4. Decide and explainThe score sets the band: allow 0-19, review 20-59, block 60-100. The three signals that moved the score most become its reasons, in plain words.

About these numbers

Synthetic data. 8,000 users over 60 days: 148,485 transactions, 1.13% of them fraud. It contains account takeovers after a SIM swap, scams with no SIM swap, genuine look-alikes such as real SIM swaps and new phones, plus new accounts and people who normally send at night. These numbers show that the method works; your own data sets the real ones.

One dataset of seven. Seven synthetic datasets were tested, this one included, with about 296,000 test transactions in all. Across the seven, the model blocked 86% to 91% of fraud, and 94% to 98% of what it blocked was fraud. The 24-hour SIM-swap rule caught 77% to 81% (80.5% in the first, smaller demo). Retraining on the same data moves the model by about one point, so read each number as a range, not an exact value.

On your data, the same pipeline recomputes these results at every retrain and prints them in its report.
