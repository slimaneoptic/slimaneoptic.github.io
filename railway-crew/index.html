Onboard crew deployment on long-distance trains: relays vs. end-to-end crews

An illustrative model of the question: how to keep onboard service continuous while every worker stays within working-hour and rest rules, without a large jump in manpower or cost. The timetable, rules and costs below are hypothetical; with real ones, the same model gives the real answer.

Three designs, same train, same rules

A daily train pair, A to B and back, 32 hours each way, with 7 stations where a crew could hand over. Service needs 6 staff onboard by day and 3 at night. Rules: at most 8 hours of work in any 24 hours, 48 a week, 8 hours of rest between duties, one day off a week.

Cost = staff paid a full week + overtime at 2× + a rest allowance per night away + the running cost of each manpower hub. Relative units; any currency.

  • Meeting the rules by adding staff costs about the same as paying overtime, because overtime disappears, but it needs 24 more people (80 instead of 56) and more berths onboard.
  • Relays meet the rules with 5 more people than today and cost 8% less. Crews work stretches of the route and hand over at H2, H4, H6; night legs carry 3 staff instead of 6. The optimizer chose those hubs out of 7 and proved the plan optimal among 130 possible crew tours.
  • The answer depends on the rules and costs, which is why pinning them down comes first (table below). With a 10-hour daily limit, end-to-end crews become cheaper than relays.

How the model works

  1. Generate every legal crew tour. From each possible base, the model lists every sequence of duties and rests that returns home and respects the working-hour rules, checked in every 24-hour window.
  2. Choose tours and hubs together. An optimizer (Google OR-Tools CP-SAT) picks how many crews run each tour and which hubs to open, so every stretch of every train has the staff it needs, at the lowest total cost.
  3. Compare designs on the same basis. Headcount includes weekly rest days; cost includes overtime, rest allowances and hub running costs.

With real data, the model would add the full timetable (crews shared across many trains and hubs, where the largest savings usually are), actual rules and contracts, existing depots and rest facilities, staff categories, and service levels by coach and time of day.
