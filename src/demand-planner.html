The four hard parts and how I'd solve them

Forecasting is the easy half. The planner earns its keep when it turns censored history into container decisions you can defend, across locations and Amazon states, and when every decision shows its inputs.

  1. Censored demand

    Estimate it with a confidence code; never "reconstruct it right"

    • Availability first, from the cheap source. Rebuild on-hand per SKU, location and day from the ledgers (receipts, shipments, adjustments, FBA inbound and outbound), then reconcile with whatever snapshots exist.
    • Flag unavailable periods from zero-sales runs against the SKU's expected rate, listing status and FBA states, not only from on-hand = 0.
    • Fill flagged periods from the SKU's own uncensored periods, scaled by seasonality and family trend (a censored-regression estimate), with a reason code.
    • Confidence codes A / B / C (snapshot-backed, ledger-only, thin history) travel with the estimate into safety stock, so an uncertain reconstruction buys more buffer, not a bolder forecast.

    Observed sales and normalized demand are both kept. Nothing missing silently becomes zero.

  2. Forecasting

    By behaviour class, chosen by rolling backtests, output as quantiles

    • Regular movers: seasonal ETS / Theta against a global gradient-boosting model with multi-year seasonality and price, promo and event features.
    • Intermittent: Croston / TSB style rates with a bootstrapped lead-time demand distribution.
    • Highly seasonal: family-level multi-year profiles reconciled down to SKUs; the short recent window only scales the level.
    • New products: attribute and family analogs with a launch curve, re-weighted weekly from early sales.
    • Champion-challenger per class, re-run at every origin; bias is tracked as a first-class metric, not a footnote.

    Every forecast is a distribution (P50, P90, P95). Safety stock needs the distribution, not the mean.

  3. Purchasing

    Containers as one optimization, with profitability guardrails

    • Forward supply projected per SKU and location: stock, open POs, lead times, demand quantiles. Reorder timing and quantity fall out of the projection.
    • Container composition as a CP-SAT model: MOQ, case pack, supplier cadence, cube and weight. The objective is margin-weighted cover to a target horizon, not units. The model decides whether to order at all and how many containers are justified; the buyer does not pick the count first.
    • Guardrails: contribution per SKU and channel in the objective; clearance, coupon and heavy-ads periods carried as demand states and excluded from the baseline; family economics checked before any child SKU is starved; strategic growth and hero stock as policy rows with their incremental capital visible.

    Velocity alone never triggers a buy. The recommendation shows the economics that justify it.

  4. Network and FBA

    Any number of nodes; Amazon inventory is five numbers, not one

    • Location-agnostic model. Nodes are rows in an effective-dated location table with eligibility and lead times; going from 2 DCs to 5 changes data, not code.
    • Reserve local non-FBA demand first, then source FBA shipments from what remains, so a marketplace spike cannot empty the websites' stock.
    • Amazon states (available, transfer, reserved/processing, inbound, unusable) each carry their own planning value and time-to-available.
    • Economics where the data exists: FBA and AWD fees, storage, placement and freight enter the replenishment decision as costs, not as afterthoughts.

    Exception-based: thousands of item-location decisions run nightly; people see the few that are economically meaningful.

Architecture: batch planner on your warehouse, thin app for actions

Three parts, each in your repositories and your Azure subscription. The planner reads canonical data, writes a dated run snapshot, and the action queue works from that snapshot, so a late warehouse load never produces a half-empty plan.

data and snapshot flowdecisions written back for the next run and the backtestfallback when the planner or the warehouse is late
  • Data contract, defined in Milestone 0: table, grain, keys, freshness SLA and owner for each input; effective dating on product relationships, locations, suppliers and policy so a backtest replays the rules that existed on that date.
  • Scale honestly: a few million order lines and a few thousand SKUs is a nightly batch on one modest VM or a scheduled Azure job. No streaming, no cluster.
  • Resilience: source-freshness checks gate each run; a missing or stale input stops the step and raises an exception instead of becoming zero; the queue keeps serving the last good snapshot.

  • All data is synthetic; the generator is smooth and the reconstruction matches its structure, so 1.6% is a lower bound, not a forecast of your result.

  • Container figures are a yardstick against the planner's own demand estimate, not realized sales.

How we prove it before you rely on it

Rolling-origin backtest of the whole policy

Not just the forecast. Replay 12 to 24 months of your history: at each origin the planner forecasts, sizes safety stock, times replenishment and composes containers using only what was known on that date (effective-dated rules included). An inventory simulation then runs those decisions forward and compares them with what your current process actually decided, SKU by SKU.

Shadow mode, then live

Champion-challenger on a set of SKU families for one or two purchasing cycles: the planner's recommendations sit next to the buyer's, nothing executes automatically, and the deltas are explained. Only after that do routine actions move to the queue, with high-value and low-confidence cases still escalating.

Judged on the metrics in your section 8

  • Forecast error and bias vs simple baselines and vs buyer overrides.
  • Stockout-adjusted demand capture and lost contribution, not gross lost sales.
  • Inventory investment, turns and forward-cover distribution, read with service level and profitable growth.
  • Excess prevented while action is still reversible: PO reduction, transfer, markdown, supplier action.
  • FBA availability and total replenishment economics, including avoidable low-stock, storage, placement and freight costs.
  • Working-capital and supplier-commercial improvements.
  • Share of routine recommendations an administrator can execute vs cases escalated to skilled review.
  • Economic outcome of accepted vs overridden recommendations, not only model accuracy.

Where I'd push back

Your overview asks for disagreement in Milestone 0. Four positions I would argue for, open to being talked out of.

  1. Censored demand is an estimation problem with stated uncertainty, not a reconstruction to get "right".No method recovers the true demand of a month with no stock. Carry a confidence code and a low/high range per SKU-period into safety stock and show a buyer the sensitivity, instead of one confident number.
  2. Ledgers before snapshots.Daily on-hand rebuilt from receipts, shipments, adjustments and FBA movements covers more history, more cheaply, than hunting for snapshots that may not exist. Reconcile against the snapshots you do have; use them to grade the ledger, not to replace it.
  3. Seasonality belongs at the family level, with multi-year history.A per-SKU seasonality index from a short window is mostly noise plus last year's stockouts. Estimate the profile on the family across years, reconcile down, and let the recent window set only the level.
  4. Keep the planner app thin.The operational tool is a queue with approvals, overrides, escalation and audit, reading from the run snapshot. Reporting stays in Power BI; data stays in the warehouse. A second pipeline or a second BI layer is the fastest way to lose the single source of truth.
